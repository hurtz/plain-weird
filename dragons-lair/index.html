Dragon's Lair

01

Before the dragon

Dragon's Lair started as a box that printed a story on a roll of paper.

Rick Dyer loved Adventure, the old text game where you type GO NORTH and read what happens. He wanted a version you could see, so he built what he called The Fantasy Machine.

The first versions were a little computer with a paper roll inside. The roll had text and hand drawn pictures on it. You pushed word buttons like NORTH, CLIMB, SWORD or OPEN, and the machine scrolled the paper to the right spot. Later versions swapped the paper for a videodisc of mostly still pictures and narration.

Nobody bought it. Dyer decided the problem was excitement, not the idea. Then he saw Don Bluth's 1982 film The Secret of NIMH and knew what was missing: real animation. He brought Bluth's studio in, his company Advanced Microcomputer Systems (later RDI Video Systems) handled the engineering, and Cinematronics built and sold the cabinets.

The budget was about $1 million, so the animators did the voices themselves. Film editor Dan Molina is Dirk. The only professional voice is the narrator in the attract mode, Michael Rye.

02

Drawing it

Every frame of Dirk was a hand painted cel, shot on film in early 1983.

The prototype disc still has the film slates in it, the clapboards held up in front of the camera before each shot. They read like a timeline. The ones I found are dated February 1 to February 15, 1983, all marked Production 107, with animator names like Pomeroy, Spafford, Wurzer and Heidi.

That puts the drawing about four months before the game hit arcades on June 19. The pipeline was a full movie studio's: storyboards, pencil animation, a pencil test to check the motion, ink and paint on clear cels, painted backgrounds, then a film camera shooting it all frame by frame. The film was mastered to laserdisc.

03

The arcade, 1983

It looked like a Disney movie you could steer, and people lined up to watch other people die.

Every other game in the arcade was built from little blocks of color. Dragon's Lair was a cartoon. It cost 50 cents, double the normal quarter, and operators paid around $4,200 for a cabinet. It made about $32 million in its first eight months.

Many arcades put a second TV on top of the cabinet so the crowd could watch. That is the screen everyone remembers. Watching was half the game, because you learned the rooms by seeing someone else die in them.

TV news crews came to film the lines. The footage below is from their tapes and from the creators' own 1983 and 1984 tapes.

04

Inside the machine

There is no graphics chip drawing Dirk. The computer's only job is to tell a disc player which frame to jump to.

1 · You move

Stick or sword. Diagonals count as their own inputs.

2 · Z80 checks

Is this the right input, inside the window, for this part of the room?

3 · Frame number

It picks where the answer lives on the disc: keep rolling, or a death clip.

4 · Seek

The laser jumps to that frame. Short hop, instant. Long hop, a pause.

5 · Screen

Video to the monitor, score to the LEDs, a beep from the sound chip.

Mirrored rooms

Points

Operator trick
Hold Up and Left while starting a game: unlimited lives

Why the players broke. A normal movie plays straight through. Dragon's Lair makes the laser sled jump all over the disc, hundreds of times an hour, all day. The PR-7820 players died fast. Cinematronics moved to the LD-V1000 and a revised board, and the last ROM, revision F, stopped shuffling the room order just to cut down the travel.

Today a board called Dexter replaces the player completely and plays a disc image. The game never knows.

The prototype even had its own screens. The early disc and ROM show plain text screens like this one, a peek at the test build before the art was final.

How the game picks your next room

The rooms sit in a table of 13 rows by 3. The game walks down the rows three times. Each time through, it picks one room you haven't played yet from each row. That's how mirrored rooms and different rooms swap places from game to game. This is the table, straight from the game's data.

Row 13 is lockedThe long elevator on pass one, its mirror on pass two, the Dragon's Lair on pass three.
Row 4 repeatsThe short elevator sits in two slots, so it can come up twice.
Deaths come backRooms you die in go on a list of up to 8. You must beat them all before the game lets you into the lair.
Very hard modeOperators can set it so five rooms get harder timing, with windows as short as a tenth of a second.

06

The cut version

Before release, the rooms had extra doors, extra choices and extra deaths. Here they are.

The prototype disc was looser. Most rooms had more than one way out. Then it was cut back to a single path per room before release; the usual story is that testers got lost. The cut came after the animation was finished, so pieces of the old rooms are still visible on the final disc if you know where to look.

A whole swamp. The prototype disc also has a green swamp with creatures on tree stumps and a treasure chest on top of a stump tower. I couldn't match it to any room in the final game.

The prototype footage was pressed onto a limited reproduction laserdisc in 2002, and fans made Enhancement ROMs that let a real cabinet play the cut rooms.

07

The family

Same trick, five very different machines.

Space Ace

1984 · Cinematronics · Don Bluth

  • Skill buttons. Cadet plays about half the scenes, Captain about two thirds, Space Ace all of them.
  • Energize. A button that turns scrawny Dexter back into Ace, sending the disc down a different branch. The first real choice in one of these games.
  • Ace's ship and motorcycle were real models, filmed and traced. A big tunnel model gave the camera a dogfight to fly through.
  • Don Bluth himself voices the villain Borf.
  • Sold mostly as a kit for Dragon's Lair cabinets, with an extra board for the skill buttons.

Time Traveler

1991 · Sega · Rick Dyer · the hologram game

  • Not really a hologram. A TV under the stage shines into a big concave mirror, which floats a real image in the air. The black cabinet hides the mirror. Same physics as the mirror toys that make a coin float.
  • Shot in San Diego with about 40 actors, mostly on green screen, so the actors could stand on the little stage.
  • A rewind button in 1991. The Time Reversal button jumps back to just before you died.
  • Hidden: with a credit in, hold both buttons and pull down. Rick Dyer dances with his son on his back.
  • Sony LDP-1450 player. Sega later reused the cabinet for a fighting game, Holosseum.

Dragon's Lair II

Drawn 1983 to the late 80s · released 1991 · Leland

  • Started right after the first game, shelved in March 1984 when the laserdisc craze crashed, finished years later.
  • It shows you the answer. A quick yellow flash marks where to go. The first game mostly made you guess.
  • Hidden golden treasures. Miss one and the game loops you back to it before it lets you finish.
  • Dirk chases Mordroc through time: Wonderland, the Garden of Eden, Beethoven's study.

Thayer's Quest

1984 · RDI · Rick Dyer

  • No joystick. A membrane QWERTY keyboard with item keys, the first on an arcade game.
  • You type your name and the speech chip says it during the game. It saved progress for the last ten players.
  • Built from Shadoan, the big story Dragon's Lair was spun out of.
  • Also made for the RDI Halcyon, a $2,500 home laserdisc console you talked to. You trained it on your voice by repeating words like BLACK MACE and CRYSTAL.

Us vs. Them

1984 · Mylstar · Warren Davis of Q*bert

  • Two layers: real aerial film on the disc, computer drawn ships and shots on top.
  • Live action alien invasion scenes on custom sets, with an orchestral score.
  • It tested well, then the laserdisc backlash hit. Orders were canceled and very few were built.
  • Warren Davis later built the video digitizing system behind Mortal Kombat and NBA Jam.

08

Making one in 2027

Keep the movie, fix the guessing, bring back the doors.

Keep

  • Hand made looking frames. The art is why people lined up.
  • A spectator view. The crowd was half the fun, so build the TV on top on purpose.
  • One input per moment.

Fix

  • Fair cues for every move, like Dragon's Lair II's flash.
  • Rewind instead of game over, like Time Traveler.
  • Wide windows by default, real 1983 timing as an arcade mode.

Bring back

  • The prototype's extra doors and flasks, so the castle becomes a map you learn.
  • Space Ace's Energize as a risk choice.
  • A narrator who says your name, like Thayer's Quest in 1984, with today's AI voices. The Halcyon's talking console finally works now.
  • A concave mirror cabinet. Still cheap, still stops people.

The movie

Netflix and Ryan Reynolds announced a live action Dragon's Lair in 2020, and James Bobin was later reported as director. Nothing released yet that I could find.

Play it now

Hypseus Singe, the maintained fork of the Daphne laserdisc emulator, runs it on Mac, Windows and Raspberry Pi with the ROMs and disc video. DirkSimple is a small open source player. Digital Leisure keeps official versions in print.
